I spent a month last summer doing something completely unrelated to my work: sketching buildings. Not digital renders, not 3D models — pencil on paper, buildings I walked past, façades of train stations, the corner of a parking structure that caught the light interestingly.

I didn't do it to become a better designer. I did it because I was tired and wanted a practice with no stakes. But it changed how I think about interfaces.

## Proportion Is Not Decoration

Architectural proportion is structural. The golden ratio and its variations were not decorative choices — they were discovered to produce buildings that felt balanced at human scale. When the width of a column relates mathematically to its height, and when that height relates to the spacing between columns, the result feels effortless without the viewer knowing why.

This is exactly what good spacing scales do in interface design. When the spacing between elements follows a consistent mathematical relationship — say, a scale of 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32 — the interface feels settled. Nothing fights for attention. The eye moves naturally.

When I sketch buildings, I'm constantly measuring. Not in absolute terms, but relationally. How does this window's width compare to its height? How does the height of the first floor compare to the second? These ratios are the invisible structure of the building's visual logic.

## Negative Space Is Not Nothing

One of the most counterintuitive lessons of architectural sketching: the space between things is as designed as the things themselves.

In a great building, the void between two columns is not leftover space. It is held space. It has weight and intention. The building breathes.

In an interface, whitespace is often treated as what you have when you run out of content. This is backwards. Generous negative space is what makes content legible. It is what creates the sense that a screen has been considered rather than filled.

The buildings I find most calming to sketch are not the most ornate. They are the ones where the emptiness feels deliberate — where the architect knew that leaving space was itself a decision.

## Rhythm and Repetition

Façades often have a rhythm: column, void, column, void, column. This repetition creates predictability, and predictability is oddly comforting. You know what to expect. Your eye can rest.

Navigation bars, card grids, list items — these are interfaces using the same principle. Repetition signals system. It tells the user: this is how we do things here. Trust it.

The danger is monotony. Architecture solves this with variation within the rhythm: a slightly wider column here, a recessed element there. Interfaces solve it with visual hierarchy — not every item in a list is the same weight, even if they all follow the same structural pattern.

## What I Brought Back

After that month of sketching, I started thinking about interfaces more spatially. Not as screens to fill, but as places to inhabit. Places have proportion. Places have rhythm. Places have held space and considered voids.

The best interfaces I've designed since feel less like products and more like rooms. I don't know if that's exactly right. But it's closer to right than what I was doing before.
